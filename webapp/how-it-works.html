How it works

From a wave of the hand to words on screen.

Three different problems — a letter is a still shape, a number is a count of fingers, a sentence is a movement over time — so Hastavāṇī uses three different techniques. All of them run inside your browser tab; the only downloads are Google's open hand- and body-tracking models, fetched once and cached.

🔤 Letters 🔢 Numbers 💬 Sentences

Letters

From camera frame to letter, in five small steps.

  1. 1

    Capture

    Your browser gives the page a live video stream after you allow camera access. Frames are read locally and never stored or sent anywhere.

  2. 2

    Find the hand

    MediaPipe Hand Landmarker locates each hand and returns 21 key points per hand: the wrist and the joints of every finger. ASL tracks one hand; ISL tracks up to two.

  3. 3

    Normalise the shape

    The points are moved so the hand is centred and scaled to a standard size. That makes recognition independent of where your hand is in the frame or how close it is to the camera.

  4. 4

    Classify

    A small neural network (a multilayer perceptron) turns the normalised points into a probability for each of the 26 letters. It is tiny enough to run in plain JavaScript on every frame.

  5. 5

    Smooth and lock in

    Single noisy frames are ignored. A letter is accepted only when the model is confident and the same answer wins a vote over the last several frames. It is then added to your word and, if speech is on, spoken.

Why landmarks?

Shape beats pixels.

An earlier version classified cropped camera images. It worked on its training photos and fell apart on new backgrounds, lighting and skin tones. Reading the hand's skeleton instead removes all of that, so the same sign looks the same to the model wherever you are.

Numbers

Counting fingers, the clever way.

Digits don't need a neural network at all. The public digit datasets didn't match the charts this site teaches, so instead of training on the wrong signs, the digit reader works like a person would: it looks at the hand's geometry — which fingers are extended, whether the thumb is out or tucked, and which fingertip the thumb is touching (that's what separates 6, 7, 8 and 9). The handshapes for most digits are the same in ASL and ISL, so one reader serves both.

Signs a little differently? Record a few samples of your own on the Math page and they're blended with the rules, just for you.

Sentences

A different problem: motion, not a pose.

A letter is one hand shape held still. A sentence is a few seconds of hands moving through space — so instead of looking at one frame, the sentence builder compares your whole recording against real recordings of every sentence it knows.

  1. 1

    Track hands and body

    Every frame records each hand's shape, and where your hands, elbows and wrists are relative to your shoulders — so signing near your face and near your chest look different, whatever your camera framing.

  2. 2

    Align the motion

    The idle start and end (hands resting in your lap) are trimmed off, then dynamic time warping lines your movement up with each reference — even if you sign faster or slower than they did.

  3. 3

    Pick, and learn you

    The closest references win and their sentences are shown as ranked guesses. Confirm or correct the result and your own recording joins the references — only in your browser.

Why these numbers are modest — and honest

The ISL recordings (ISL-CSLTR) have only about five takes per sentence, and people sign the same sentence very differently: even two signers filmed in the same room on the same phone match each other only about 7% of the time. We report the new-signer number because that's what you experience. A trained neural network was tried first and did worse; matching against real examples copes better with this little data.

The fix: teach it your signing

Your own earlier recording is by far the closest match to how you sign. After each result, press Correct — remember it or pick the right sentence and Save as this. Two or three takes of the sentences you use most make a real difference.

ASL sentences: built from real word clips

No public dataset records fixed ASL sentences several times over, so the 101 ASL sentences are built differently: each is written in ASL word order (for example YOUR NAME WHAT), and its references are chains of real word clips from the WLASL dataset — about six different signers per word — with the pause around each word cut out.

With signers it had never seen, it picked the right sentence 77% of the time (90% in its top three) — but those tests were also chained word clips. Fluent signing blends words together, so expect less live, sign each word clearly, and treat ASL sentences as an early experiment.
